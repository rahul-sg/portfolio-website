LLM Evaluation Framework

Honda Research Institute and 99P Labs

An evaluation framework for LLM-generated summaries, built to be reference-free and domain-aware rather than dependent on a gold answer. I led the evaluation research on a four-person capstone with Honda Research Institute and 99P Labs, as one of twelve students selected for the programme.

The problem with the usual metrics

ROUGE, BLEU, and BERTScore reward surface similarity to a reference. They do not see whether a summary is factually grounded in the source, whether it covers what matters, or whether it is structured the way a reader in that domain expects. For summarisation of dense technical material, those are the only things that matter.

Human review measures the right thing but does not scale, and it is expensive enough that it stops being run. So the question is not whether to use an LLM as a judge, but how to make an LLM judge you can actually trust.

The loop

A judge scores the current summary against a rubric selected for the detected domain, deterministic signals are computed from the slides themselves, and a controller decides whether the run has converged. If not, the summary is refined and a pairwise comparison decides whether the rewrite actually replaces the current best, which stops a bad rewrite from propagating.

Scoring is hybrid on purpose. The rubric judge catches the things only a language model notices, and the deterministic signals, such as length error, section coverage, and lexical grounding against the slide text, catch the things a language model will happily agree with itself about.

Knowing when to stop

Iterative refinement raises a question nobody asks until the compute bill arrives: when is a run finished? Rather than a fixed number of rounds, a decision table runs over several signals, including the composite quality score, the deterministic signals, and the trend across iterations. It enforces a minimum of four iterations before it is allowed to stop and a hard maximum of twelve so a run cannot loop away.

It reports a stop state rather than a bare number: pass, borderline, or stalled. A run that plateaued at a mediocre score is a different result from one that converged, and collapsing them into a single figure hides the case you most need to see. At the end, a best-of-last-three safeguard picks the final summary, because the last rewrite is not reliably the best one.

What it scored

Across seven cross-domain lecture sets and three random seeds, the grand mean final score is 0.845 with a standard deviation of 0.017. The tuned policy beat the legacy one on all seven lectures, averaging 0.076 higher. The hardest set was the theory-heavy humanities lecture at 0.825, which is the result I would have predicted and was glad to see the system agree with.

For validation I built a human-labelled faithfulness set, 50 samples across all seven lectures, and measured how well the automated score tracked it. Spearman against human faithfulness is 0.808, and against the hallucination signal it is -0.692, in the direction you would want. That correlation, not the score itself, is the number that says whether the evaluator works.

Why the headline number went down

An earlier version of this system reported an average final score of 0.962 against human reference summaries. The later reference-free pipeline reports 0.845, and the drop is the point rather than a regression.

The first number was measured against a gold summary on an easier task. The second is measured without a reference, on a harder rubric, with a hallucination penalty applied and robustness checked across seeds. A number that falls when you make the measurement honest is the number worth reporting.

Honest limits

Seven lectures are not enough for strong statistical claims about domain behaviour or stopping stability, so the results are descriptive evidence rather than a benchmark.

The same model family generates and judges. That risks style alignment between generator and evaluator and probably inflates rubric scores relative to what an independent model or a human would give. It is the single biggest threat to the result and the first thing I would change, by swapping in a judge from a different family and re-running the calibration.

The hallucination signal is a lexical grounding heuristic, so it can penalise a faithful but heavily paraphrased summary. It is a risk flag, not a measurement of fabrication, and the calibration work exists precisely because it needed one.
