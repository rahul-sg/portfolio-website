Fantasy Edge

Fantasy football help for ESPN and Sleeper leagues

A live fantasy football service for ESPN and Sleeper leagues, built on a gradient-boosted projection model validated against a season it never saw, interval estimates that land where they claim to, and a correlated Monte Carlo that turns all of it into which lineup actually wins the week.

What it is

Edge connects to ESPN and Sleeper fantasy leagues and answers the questions that come up every week: who to start, who to pick up, what to bid, and who to trade for. It's live at edgefantasy.us, invite-only for a group of friends, with a sample league open to anyone.

Every number on the screen is a distribution, not a point estimate. Each one was checked against a season the model never saw, and the parts that couldn't prove they helped are switched off.

The projection model, and what it's measured against

A LightGBM model over 79 features, trained on 52,764 skill-position games. Four heads: a mean regressor plus p10, p50 and p90 quantile regressors with the quantiles forced into order. Features are strictly backward-looking, and the same function builds training rows and inference rows, so a traded player inherits his new team’s context instead of leaking his old one.

Validation splits by time, not at random. Train through season N minus one, score the whole of season N, then refit. Random splits would let the model see week 12 while predicting week 11 of the same season, which would flatter it badly.

For a fantasy projection, the baseline to beat is a player’s own season average, because that's what you would use if you built nothing. It beats that at every position on RMSE in both tested seasons.

Why RMSE

RMSE weighs big misses more heavily than small ones, and these projections get summed into a lineup total, so one catastrophic miss costs more than several near misses. MAE rewards predicting the median, which flatters a season average at positions whose scoring is driven by touchdowns.

You can see the difference in the numbers. On MAE the 2025 running backs look like a tie, 4.27 against the baseline’s 4.25. On RMSE the same games are 5.97 against 6.30. Picking the metric after seeing both would be the easiest way to lie here, so the reasoning is written down and the losing metric is reported too.

Making a learned improvement prove itself

The projection shown is a blend of ESPN, Rotowire and the model. The obvious move is to learn the mixing weights from which source has been most accurate, and that move is a trap: a mix fitted on past results will always score better on those same results.

So a learned mix only replaces the hand-set default if it beats it on held-out games in at least 80% of 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples. Running backs and receivers cleared that bar. Quarterbacks, tight ends, kickers and defences did not, and still run on the defaults. Kickers came out at 55%, which is a coin flip wearing a number.

Four of six positions rejecting my own improvement is the result I would point at first. It's the part of the system that decides what is allowed to be true.

Then checking itself against its own live output

Everything above is checked against history. In production there's a harder question: are the projections it's making right now running high or low? So the system keeps a scorecard. Before every kickoff it logs its own final projection for every rostered player, in every league, next to what that player went on to score.

Every Tuesday it measures, per position, whether this season’s projections ran high or low for the players who actually played, and scales the coming week by that much. It shows up on the player page as a Learned factor. Nothing moves before 60 games. The measured correction is shrunk toward doing nothing until roughly 400 games stand behind it, and it's never allowed past 12%. A player who appears in four leagues counts once, not four times.

Two guards matter more than the fit itself. The correction is kept only if, fitted without the latest finished week, it would have improved that week’s projections; when it wouldn't have, it's thrown away and nothing changes. And every fit divides out whatever correction was in force when each projection was made, so it always measures the raw model and corrections can never compound on each other. That second one is the quieter trap: a system that corrects its own corrected output will happily walk away from reality while every individual step looks reasonable.

That closes the loop. The model has to beat a baseline on a season it never saw before it ships, and then has to keep proving it against its own live output every week after.

Uncertainty that was measured, not assumed

A questionable tag isn't a coin flip, and guessing what it means would undo the calibration work. So it was measured: every official injury report since 2016 next to the snap counts that followed. A player listed questionable who practised fully on Friday plays 89% of the time. Limited, 76%. Did not practise, 55%. Doubtful, 1%.

It splits further by injury, by position and by role, with small groups shrunk toward their broader group and position applied on the log-odds scale. Questionable quarterbacks play 52% of the time against 74% for receivers and tight ends. A hamstring is 63% where an illness is 77%.

One thing that went wrong there was a data problem, not a modelling one, and it's the kind that hides. Early in the week the app was still showing last week’s designation, because NFL.com keeps the previous report up until each team’s practice week starts. A team’s report now counts only from four and a half days before its own kickoff, and a player still listed out from last week is judged on this week’s evidence instead of a flat zero: across 966 player-weeks, someone ruled out the previous week went on to play 1% of the time after another missed practice, 41% after a limited one and 91% after a full one.

The sharpest finding is about role, not health. A starting quarterback on the report after a full practice plays 96% of the time; a fill-in quarterback in the same situation, 83%. The fill-in isn't less healthy. He loses the job when the starter recovers, and the injury report is just where that shows up. Measuring the right cohort mattered more than any model choice here.

Picking the lineup

Projections are an input, not an answer. Each player becomes a gamma distribution matched to his blended mean and the model’s quantile spread, multiplied by a weighted coin flip for whether he suits up at all.

The matchup is then played out 12,000 times with the players correlated through a Gaussian copula: teammates share a team factor so a quarterback and his receiver rise together, both sides of a game share a game factor for shootouts, and a defence moves against the offence it's facing. Treating players as independent would understate the variance badly, which is exactly the error that makes a confident-looking lineup wrong.

The lineup is then chosen by win probability, not projected points, solved at six risk settings from floor-leaning to ceiling-leaning, with slots assigned exactly by the Hungarian algorithm under each league’s own rules. Underdogs get handed ceiling, favourites get floor. It only abandons the highest-points lineup when the gain is worth at least 0.4 percentage points of win probability, because churn with no edge is still churn.

The opponent is simulated with the lineup they have actually set, not the perfect one. Assuming every rival plays optimally would make you look like a bigger underdog than you are, and would push every recommendation toward desperate moves.

The decision that made the whole thing worth building

A star listed questionable usually gets benched out of fear. That's normally wrong. If his backup is still swappable once the inactive list drops ninety minutes before kickoff, keeping him in is worth p times the starter plus one minus p times the backup, which beats benching him whenever he is the better player when he plays.

So Edge builds the lineup valuing him as if he plays, works out the exact swaps to make if he sits (including chains, like moving a receiver to FLEX to free a slot), and scores that plan inside the simulation by applying each swap only in the runs where that player didn't suit up. Then it sorts every change into do it now, wait for the game-time call, or decide now because the backup’s game starts first and waiting isn't an option.

It only works because the play probability is right and the simulation is correlated, so both of those had to exist first.

Shipping it to other people

Running this for yourself is one thing. Running it for friends means invite-only sign-in through Clerk, a membership per person per league, daily caps on the Claude-backed features, spend tracked per day and per feature and per person, and an admin page for people, jobs and server health.

It also means holding other people’s credentials. ESPN private leagues need the user’s own session cookies, so they are encrypted at rest, not stored as plain text. The API accepts only its own origin, and the Docker build context excludes the data directory and the environment file outright, so neither can reach the builder.

The engine runs on Fly.io and the site on Vercel. The machine sizing is written down with its reasoning: five leagues on 1 GB left about 180 MB spare, which meant Tuesday’s retraining would have run on swap, so it runs on 2 GB with swap as the backstop instead.

Limits

The calibration was measured on the 2025 and 2026 seasons. Those are two seasons, not twenty, and a year with an unusual injury pattern could move it.

Nothing here predicts injuries before they happen or calls every breakout. The advantage is narrower than that: reading practice reports and line moves as they land, using role signals that move before box scores do, carrying uncertainty instead of collapsing it, and knowing what every rival in your league needs and can spend.

ESPN has no public API, so league data comes from unofficial endpoints and the news from ordinary public pages, cached and rate-limited per host. That's fine for personal use, and it's why this is invite-only and not a product. Turning it into one would mean licensed feeds.
